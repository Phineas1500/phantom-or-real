Phantom or Real · a plain-language tour · July 4–6, 2026

The model knows when it’s about to be wrong. That knowledge doesn’t steer it.

What we found inside Gemma 3 27B, why three other labs just validated half of it, and the two experiments hardening the other half — the first of which passed this afternoon.

The one-picture version: the model carries an accurate internal “am I getting this right?” signal (the gauge) and a separate internal variable that actually decides the answer (the lever). They are physically different pieces of machinery, and only the second one moves the output.

The experiment, in one card

We give Gemma 3 27B small logic puzzles. Each puzzle lays out a tiny invented taxonomy of made-up creatures, then shows a few observations, and asks the model to propose the rule (“hypothesis”) that best explains what was observed:

Given

Every zumpus is a wumpus.
Every wumpus is a yumpus.
Stella is a zumpus.

Observed

Stella is bright.
Stella is not dull.

The model’s job

Propose the best explanation — e.g. “Every zumpus is bright.” Picking the wrong creature-level (“every yumpus…”) is the classic failure.

Sometimes the model nails these; often it doesn’t. While it reads each puzzle we record its internal activations — the millions of numbers flowing through its layers — and ask two questions: does the model internally know whether it’s about to succeed? And if so, can we grab that internal signal and use it to fix mistakes?

Finding 1 — There’s a gauge: the internals broadcast success in advance

A very simple detector (a “linear probe” — essentially a weighted sum of the activations) can predict whether the answer will be correct before the model writes a single word. And it’s not a fluke of one spot: the signal is readable at many layers, in two different models, and it survives scrambling all the creature names.

Finding 2 — But it’s a gauge, not a lever

The obvious next move: if the model knows it’s going wrong, push that internal signal toward “correct” and fix the mistake from inside. We tried that for months, roughly ten different ways. It never worked.

So we ran the decisive test in the other direction: instead of pushing the signal, we deleted it — surgically removed the correctness-readout direction from the activations. If the signal were part of the machinery, the model should get worse. It didn’t budge. Meanwhile, deleting a same-sized control direction wrecked the model:

That’s the “gauge” result. A car’s fuel gauge faithfully reports what’s in the tank, but bending the needle doesn’t add gasoline — and removing the gauge entirely doesn’t stop the engine. The correctness signal is a downstream report of how the computation is going, not a knob that controls it. The technical word we use is epiphenomenal: readable, accurate, and causally inert.

Finding 3 — We found the actual lever

If the readout isn’t the cause, what is? Behavioral detective work pointed at one variable: which concept the model decides to build its hypothesis around. It usually knows all the right ingredients — but it locks onto the wrong creature-level early, while still reading the puzzle. Just telling it the right concept (“Hint: the hypothesis should be about zumpuses”) sends accuracy from near-floor to near-ceiling:

Then the causal step. We run the model twice on the same puzzle — once with the hint, once without — and copy the hinted run’s internal state into the unhinted run, but only at the handful of token positions where the target concept is mentioned:

Everyzumpusisawumpus. Everywumpusisayumpus. Stellaisazumpus. Stellaisbright.

The repair only works when the hinted state is written at the highlighted tokens — the places where the concept itself appears. Writing it at random positions does nothing. The “which concept am I focused on” variable physically lives there.

That last bar is the headline. The entire “focused on the right concept” state compresses into 8 directions out of 5,376 — and those 8 directions are geometrically unrelated to the directions the correctness probe reads (overlap ≈ 0.0002, lower than random chance). The gauge and the lever are different machinery, in different parts of the space.

Why now: three labs just published the gauge. Nobody has the lever.

A deep literature sweep on July 4 (18 sources, every claim independently verified) found that in the last three months, three groups published the first half of this story — models that visibly know when they’re wrong, and steering that fails to fix it:

arXiv 2605.05715 “Decodable but Not Corrected” Found the gauge; concluded the signal is entangled with computation because their deletion hurt the model (−3.6pp). Ours didn’t — a directly testable disagreement.
arXiv 2604.13068 Detection without correction Same readable-but-unsteerable pattern in 7 different models. Breadth, no mechanism.
arXiv 2605.23315 Coins “epiphenomenal correctness” Names the phenomenon — the very term we use — but stops at the observation.

All three walked up to the same wall — the signal is there but you can’t steer it — and stopped. None of them found the causal variable, the token positions where it lives, or a compact recipe that repairs behavior. That’s our unique contribution, and it’s why the current objective is: harden the two headline claims, then write the paper fast.

The two shields: both passed

Both experiments had their pass/fail rules written down and committed before we looked at any data, so we can’t fool ourselves after the fact.

Shield 1 — Is the 8-direction recipe genuinely special? passed · strongest branch

A skeptical reviewer’s best attack: “maybe adding any big vector at those tokens fixes the model — your 8 directions are irrelevant.” So we built a ladder that removes one ingredient at a time: the real recipe, then just its average component, then random directions dressed up to match it in size, position, and rank, then pure noise. Both halves of the data are in, pooled over 26 puzzles:

Shield 2 — Delete the entire gauge at once passed

Finding 2 deleted the readout one direction at a time. The rival paper’s “entanglement” story predicts that deleting all of it — nine directions across all five readable layers simultaneously, at every token — should finally hurt. Our story predicted it still wouldn’t — and it didn’t (+0.047, CI straddling zero; matched random stacks: −0.38). The branches we registered in advance:

If accuracy doesn’t move The gauge is epiphenomenal at full strength — the strongest version of Finding 2, and the rival’s entanglement explanation is excluded in our setting.
If it breaks (beyond matched random) Our first necessity result: the readable stack is load-bearing after all, and Findings 2–3 get carefully rescoped. Painful but publishable — and pre-registered.

New this evening: a door opened — steering without the answer key

The compression result raised an obvious question: is this a general way to make models more accurate on hard tasks? There’s a catch in the recipe — the 8 directions are shared across puzzles, but how far to push along each one is computed from the puzzle’s own hinted run. The repair still consumes the answer key. So we asked the make-or-break question: is that per-puzzle information already present in the failing run’s own activations, before any hint exists?

It is. A simple linear readout, trained on 12 puzzles and tested on the 13th, predicts the 8 repair coordinates from the unhinted states alone:

Translation: the hint doesn’t inject the commitment information — it surfaces information the failing model already carries but fails to act on. That is the ingredient a real accuracy method would need.

The follow-up ran overnight — and failed in the most useful way landed · informative failure

The predicted-coefficient test came back a clean FAIL: the predictor worked (it read the right coordinates from the failing run), but predicted coordinates repaired no better than deliberately scrambled ones. The reason turned out to be the discovery: the experiment’s reference frame (built from development puzzles) transfers weakly, and within it, which subspace you write into matters enormously — the exact coordinates inside it barely matter. Every arm writing into the true 8-direction subspace repaired; content in random subspaces (from the specificity test) never did.

Overnight, the story kept turning

That reframing spawned two more pre-registered experiments the same night. First: do the model’s natural successes and failures — no hints anywhere — differ along the lever’s 8 directions? No: a probe reading only those directions can’t tell natural right-bound from wrong-bound runs any better than random directions can (0.70–0.72 vs a random-slice bar of 0.72). But second: take the average difference between natural successes and failures, squeeze it into the lever subspace, amplify it, and write it into failing runs — and accuracy jumps +0.341, the same size as our original recipe, from a direction computed without a single hint. Two adversarial review rounds (verdicts upgraded to Major Revision, twice) then demanded the decisive control: is it really the correct-vs-wrong structure carrying that effect, or would any statistically similar vector do? Both reviewers bet against the labels mattering.

Both shields held — the program closed 8 for 8

The full-gauge erasure finished with the registered prediction intact: delete the entire readable correctness signal — every layer, every position — and the model doesn’t blink (+0.047, CI straddling zero), while matched random deletions of the same size are catastrophic (−0.38). The deployment questions closed too: firing the repair on puzzles the model was already getting right helps (+0.27), but the method still needs to be told where to write — every answer-free position policy we pre-named failed, and the mechanism readout explains why: the vector is a commitment command. The content is the verb; the marked concept is the object.

New: a second brain has the same anatomy — with different measurements

Everything above happened inside one model, Gemma 3 27B. The obvious objection: maybe this is one model’s quirk. So we ported the whole pipeline — pre-registered, decision rules written before each unblinding — to a different 27B model from a different lab (Qwen3.5). The port landed in three moves. First, the same failure mode exists: unhinted, Qwen solves 19% of the hard puzzles; merely mentioning the right concept (not the answer) lifts it past 80%. Second, the same kind of carrier exists: a concept-position state that repairs failing runs when transplanted — but it lives deeper in the network (two-thirds of the way up, vs halfway in Gemma). Third, the same low-rank channel exists — and here the first attempt missed. Eight directions, Gemma’s number, wasn’t significant in Qwen. A registered follow-up swept the rank and found why: Qwen’s channel is wider. Sixteen directions clear every bar (+0.117, and +0.154 against matched random-subspace controls); by 64 directions you recover 95% of the full carrier while random 64-direction bundles at the same strength do nothing. Same anatomy, different measurements: depth 0.67 vs 0.48, rank ~16 vs 8. The motif is cross-model; the coordinates are model-specific. The answer-free repair question took three more registered experiments to resolve, and the resolution is the best kind: it works in Qwen too — but inside out. In Gemma, the raw natural-difference vector did nothing until projected into the tiny channel (+0.04 → +0.34). In Qwen, the raw vector repairs (+0.12, CI clear of zero) and projecting it destroys the effect — because Qwen smears both its channel and its content across many directions instead of concentrating them. The full control battery passed overnight: scramble the correct/wrong labels and the same-geometry vector turns null-to-harmful (real-vs-scrambled +0.18); flip the direction and it reliably hurts (the fifth pre-registered prediction confirmed in this program). One genuine mystery ships to the next paper: Qwen’s success/failure difference isn’t even linearly readable where the repair channel lives (a probe there scores 0.50 — coin-flip; in Gemma, 0.81) — yet writing it there repairs. Where Qwen actually keeps its self-knowledge is an open hunt.

Then what?

Every claim in the paper is now hardened or honestly rescoped, two adversarial review rounds survived, twelve pre-registered verdicts on the books.
